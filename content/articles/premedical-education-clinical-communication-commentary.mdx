Ask any premedical student what they spend most of their time doing, and the answer is rarely "practicing how to deliver difficult news" or "studying the ethics of surrogate decision-making." It is, overwhelmingly, studying for the MCAT and optimizing a GPA. That is a rational response to the incentives the system creates — but it is worth asking what those incentives leave out.

## What the Curriculum Measures

The premedical curriculum, as currently structured at most institutions, is a filter built almost entirely around standardized academic performance: organic chemistry, physics, biochemistry, and a four-section standardized exam that has only an indirect relationship to the skills clinicians describe as most important in daily practice — listening carefully, tolerating uncertainty, and communicating clearly under emotional pressure.

<Callout type="note" title="Scope of This Commentary">
This is an argument, not a study. It draws on published critiques of premedical curricula and general medical education literature, and reflects the author's own experience as a current premedical student rather than original data collection.
</Callout>

None of this is to say scientific competence is unimportant — a physician's clinical reasoning depends on real command of biochemistry and physiology. The concern is narrower: that the current selection and preparation pipeline treats communication and ethical reasoning as skills to be picked up later, informally, rather than as core competencies developed with the same rigor as organic chemistry.

## The Informal Curriculum Problem

Where communication training does exist before medical school, it is frequently informal: a volunteering placement, a shadowing experience, a single required ethics course taken to satisfy a distribution requirement rather than out of curricular design. Contrast this with the hundreds of hours spent on MCAT-aligned coursework and practice exams, and the asymmetry is stark.

Medical schools are aware of this gap — hence the growing emphasis on interview formats like the Multiple Mini Interview, which explicitly probes communication and ethical reasoning during admissions. But screening for a skill at the admissions gate is a poor substitute for developing it during the years students spend preparing to reach that gate.

## What Would a Better Pipeline Look Like

A more balanced premedical preparation might integrate structured communication practice — not just shadowing, but supervised, feedback-driven practice in delivering information, eliciting patient concerns, and reasoning through ethical conflicts — as a graded component of premedical coursework, not an extracurricular afterthought.

<Callout type="educational" title="An Existing Model">
Some medical schools already run structured longitudinal clinical skills courses from the first year onward. Extending a lighter version of this model earlier, into premedical education, is a natural next step rather than an untested idea.
</Callout>

## A Modest Proposal

None of this requires abandoning rigorous science preparation. It requires treating communication and ethical reasoning as competencies with the same institutional seriousness — structured coursework, real feedback, assessed practice — that organic chemistry already receives. Until that happens, the profession will keep selecting for people who test well and hoping the rest is acquired somewhere along the way.
