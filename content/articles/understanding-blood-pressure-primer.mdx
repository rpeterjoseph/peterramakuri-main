A blood pressure reading is one of the most common measurements in medicine, and one of the least understood by the people it is measured on. This guide explains what the two numbers mean and how they are classified, written for students and general readers.

## Two Numbers, Two Phases of the Heartbeat

Every blood pressure reading has two numbers, written as systolic over diastolic (for example, 120/80 mmHg).

<Callout type="educational" title="Systolic and Diastolic, Defined">
Systolic pressure is the pressure in the arteries during ventricular contraction, when the heart is actively pumping blood out. Diastolic pressure is the pressure in the arteries during ventricular relaxation, when the heart is filling for the next beat.
</Callout>

Both numbers matter. Systolic pressure reflects how forcefully the heart ejects blood and how stiff the large arteries are; diastolic pressure reflects the resistance of smaller vessels and how well the arterial system maintains pressure between beats.

## Why a Single Reading Can Mislead

Blood pressure varies throughout the day in response to activity, stress, temperature, caffeine, and even the anxiety of being measured in a clinical setting — a well-documented phenomenon sometimes called "white coat" elevation. This is why single readings are treated cautiously, and repeated or out-of-office measurement (home monitoring or ambulatory monitoring) is often recommended before a diagnosis of hypertension is made.

## Current Classification

Guideline thresholds have shifted over time as evidence about cardiovascular risk at lower pressures has accumulated. A commonly cited classification (based on U.S. guidelines) is summarized below.

| Category | Systolic (mmHg) | Diastolic (mmHg) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal | Less than 120 | Less than 80 |
| Elevated | 120–129 | Less than 80 |
| Stage 1 Hypertension | 130–139 | 80–89 |
| Stage 2 Hypertension | 140 or higher | 90 or higher |

<Callout type="clinical" title="This Table Is Not a Diagnosis">
Classification tables are a starting point for population-level risk stratification, not a substitute for individualized clinical assessment. A single elevated reading does not, by itself, establish a diagnosis of hypertension. If you are concerned about your blood pressure, speak with a licensed healthcare provider.
</Callout>

## Why It Matters Over Time

Sustained elevated pressure increases mechanical stress on arterial walls, accelerating processes such as atherosclerosis (discussed in detail in [this literature review](/writing/atherosclerosis-molecular-mechanisms-review)) and increasing the workload on the heart itself, which over years can contribute to structural changes in the heart muscle. This is why blood pressure control is one of the most consistently emphasized targets in cardiovascular prevention guidelines.

## Practical Takeaways

Blood pressure should generally be measured after several minutes of rest, seated, with the arm supported at heart level, and ideally on more than one occasion before drawing conclusions. Home monitoring, using a validated device and correct technique, is increasingly recommended as a complement to clinic measurements — but interpretation of results should always be done in partnership with a healthcare provider.
